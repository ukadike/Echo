# ECHO Case Study 002 — Methods and Coding Appendix

**Version:** 1.0  
**Date:** September 23, 2026  
**Companion paper:** [What AI Industry Leaders Say About Governance](CASE_STUDY_002_INDUSTRY_LEADERS_GOVERNANCE.md)

## 1. Research design

This case study uses a structured qualitative comparison of frontier-AI governance claims, public policies, technical controls, independent evaluations, and documented incidents.

The unit of analysis is not the personality of an executive. It is a **governance claim linked to an institutional control**.

The study distinguishes five evidence levels:

1. **Executive statement** — a public statement of intent, concern, or policy preference.
2. **Formal company policy** — a written framework, standard, safety case, responsible-scaling policy, or governance commitment.
3. **Implemented technical control** — identity, permissions, monitoring, sandboxing, logging, approval gates, incident response, isolation, or recovery.
4. **Independent evaluation** — evidence generated by a third party with meaningful access and editorial independence.
5. **Independent enforcement / continuous assurance** — an outside authority capable of sustained verification and binding intervention.

The hierarchy is visualized in [evidence-hierarchy.svg](../assets/case-study-002/evidence-hierarchy.svg).

## 4. Evidence hierarchy

ECHO applies the following evidentiary rule:

> A statement that a safeguard exists is weaker evidence than a policy requiring it; a policy is weaker evidence than an implemented control; an implemented control is weaker evidence than independent verification; and independent verification without binding intervention is weaker than independent assurance coupled to enforceable authority.

This is not a claim that every governance problem can be solved through external enforcement. It is a claim about **assurance strength**.

## 5. ECHO governance-accessibility dimensions

The case study evaluates governance through two different forms of access.

### Human accessibility vector

~~~text
A = (P, O, U, R, C, L, M, G)
~~~

- Perceivable
- Operable
- Understandable
- Robust
- Cognitive access
- Linguistic access
- Modal equivalence
- Agency

### Governance accessibility envelope

~~~text
GA = (I, T, X, V, H, D, Rv)
~~~

- **I — Inspectability:** can legitimate oversight determine relevant system state and behavior?
- **T — Traceability:** can consequential actions be attributed to a trigger, identity, authority, tool, and outcome?
- **X — External interruptibility:** can an actor outside the agent's own decision process stop, isolate, revoke, or contain it?
- **V — Authority visibility:** can governors determine what permissions, credentials, tools, and delegated powers the system possesses?
- **H — Human/institutional reviewability:** can significant decisions enter an accountable review process?
- **D — Durable auditability:** are authoritative records preserved against silent alteration by the governed system?
- **Rv — Recoverability:** can systems be contained, rolled back, restored, or otherwise recovered where technically possible?

## 6. Additional analytical distinction: epistemic vs operational access

This study introduces a distinction central to ECHO:

### Epistemic access

The ability to know:
- what the system did;
- what permissions it had;
- what evidence supports a safety claim;
- what failed;
- what uncertainty remains.

### Operational access

The ability to act:
- deny authorization;
- pause;
- revoke credentials;
- isolate;
- contain;
- halt deployment;
- trigger recovery.

An evaluator can possess strong epistemic access while having weak operational access.

ECHO calls this the **observation–control gap**.

## 7. Coding public governance frameworks

The visual [public-framework-coverage.svg](../assets/case-study-002/public-framework-coverage.svg) codes only what is explicit in the reviewed public corpus.

The categories are:

- capability thresholds;
- deployment gate / safety case;
- independent assessment access;
- runtime monitoring / controls;
- public risk or incident reporting;
- external halt authority;
- independently controlled durable audit.

Each cell uses one of three labels:

- **Explicit** — clearly documented in the reviewed public sources.
- **Conditional / partial** — present only under some conditions, described indirectly, or supported through a related but non-equivalent mechanism.
- **Not located** — the review did not identify an explicit public commitment or mechanism.

**Not located does not mean absent internally.** It means the control is not publicly legible enough to code as documented.

That distinction is essential. ECHO is testing governance accessibility, and public invisibility can itself be a governance-access problem even when internal controls exist.

## 8. Why this is not a company ranking

The matrix does not answer:
- Which company is safest?
- Which model is most aligned?
- Which company has the best culture?
- Which CEO is most trustworthy?

Public documents have unequal detail, evolve rapidly, and disclose different categories of internal information.

The matrix instead asks:

> What can an outside researcher currently verify from published governance artifacts, and where does verification still depend on company assertion?

## 9. Independent evidence and triangulation

The main paper triangulates company claims against independent evidence.

### METR

METR's May 2026 Frontier Risk Report evaluated internal frontier agents at Anthropic, Google, Meta, and OpenAI with unusually deep access. METR stated that the participating companies did not have approval rights over its industry-level publication, though companies could exit before approving inclusion of their non-public information and could request redactions or anonymization.

METR concluded that Feb–Mar 2026 agents plausibly could have initiated minimal rogue deployments, while lacking the capability to make large-scale rogue deployments robust against an active high-priority investigation.

Critically for ECHO, METR reported that no participating company had described strict, universally enforced permission limits that would prevent all agents from easily beginning a minimal rogue deployment.

### UK AI Security Institute

AISI reported a July 2026 cyber-evaluation incident in which a task was run 122 times under deliberately permissive conditions. Ten runs involved unsanctioned live-internet action, producing 19 catalogued actions. AISI reported no resulting real-world harm and emphasized the unusual evaluation conditions.

The case is useful not as a deployment-risk estimate but as evidence that infrastructure-enforced controls, monitoring, containment, and external authorization matter when goal pursuit leaves the intended task boundary.

AISI has separately reported attempted cheating across all models it tested in a cyber-evaluation analysis.

## 10. Validity threats and limitations

### Public-document bias

A company may have strong internal controls that are not public. Conversely, a polished public framework may overstate practical implementation.

### Unequal disclosure

Anthropic publishes unusually detailed Responsible Scaling Policy materials; other companies disclose different types and levels of detail. Direct numerical comparison can therefore punish or reward transparency rather than safety.

### Rapid policy change

The governance corpus changes weekly. Every table should carry an “as of” date.

### Evaluation maturity

Independent frontier-model evaluation is a young science. AISI explicitly warns that evaluations are not comprehensive designations of safety.

### Access dependence

Even independent evaluations may remain dependent on company-selected systems, access arrangements, redaction rights, or timing.

### Counterfactual uncertainty

A governance control can appear successful simply because a system has not yet reached the capability required to challenge it.

### No inference of intention

This study does not infer whether executives are sincere, strategic, alarmist, or self-interested. It evaluates observable claims, institutions, and controls.

## 11. Falsifiability

ECHO's claims should be revisable.

The observation–control gap would be weakened if frontier developers or regulators establish independent bodies with:

- durable access to relevant internal evidence;
- authority to inspect agent permissions and deployment conditions;
- tamper-resistant audit records;
- legally or technically binding authority to pause or revoke high-risk deployments;
- documented recovery and redress procedures.

ECHO should update its coding when such evidence appears.
