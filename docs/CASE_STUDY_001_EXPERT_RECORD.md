## Editorial note

The September 18, 2026 AI Evaluator Forum document is **one collective public letter**, not a set of separate letters authored by each signatory.

ECHO therefore separates:

1. what the collective letter actually says;
2. who signed it;
3. each expert's independently verifiable public work relevant to the ECHO test.

This prevents ECHO from attributing language or policy positions to a signatory that appear only in the collective document or in someone else's reporting.

The authoritative signatory list is maintained by the AI Evaluator Forum:
https://aievaluatorforum.org/initiatives/embedded-evaluation-letter

## Collective letter

**Title:** Minimum Conditions for Embedding Evaluators  
**Published:** September 18, 2026  
**Signatories:** 100+  
**Publisher:** AI Evaluator Forum

The letter calls for independent third-party evaluators to be embedded within frontier AI companies and establishes minimum conditions around independence, plural expertise, transparency, protection from retaliation, and employee-level access.

It explicitly states that embedded evaluation is not a substitute for broader external oversight.

## Selected notable signatories named in ECHO Case Study 001

### Dr. Joy Buolamwini

**Role on the 2026 letter:** Signatory; Founder, Algorithmic Justice League.

Joy Buolamwini is particularly relevant to ECHO because her work predates the 2026 embedded-evaluator letter and directly addresses algorithmic auditing, human agency, consent, discrimination, redress, and institutional accountability.

#### Algorithmic Justice League

AJL describes its mission as moving AI toward **equitable and accountable** systems. Its governance principles include:

- **Affirmative consent** — people should have meaningful choice about whether and how they interact with AI.
- **Meaningful transparency** — people should be able to understand how AI systems are created and deployed and what they can and cannot do.
- **Continuous oversight and accountability** — institutions should be monitored and held accountable beyond self-regulation, and affected people should have access to redress.
- **Actionable critique** — evaluation should be used to change harmful practices, not simply document them.

Primary source:
https://www.ajl.org/about

#### Who Audits the Auditors?

Buolamwini co-authored **Who Audits the Auditors? Recommendations from a field scan of the algorithmic auditing ecosystem** with Sasha Costanza-Chock and Inioluwa Deborah Raji.

The research is especially important to the 2026 letter because it examined the algorithmic-auditing ecosystem itself and proposed concrete accountability measures, including:

1. independent audits against clearly defined standards;
2. notice when people are subject to algorithmic decision-making;
3. disclosure of key audit findings for peer review;
4. consideration of real-world harms and standardized incident reporting;
5. direct participation by stakeholders most likely to be harmed;
6. formal evaluation and possible accreditation of auditors.

Primary source:
https://www.ajl.org/auditors

#### Written testimony to the U.S. Commission on Civil Rights

In March 2024, Buolamwini submitted written testimony on the federal use of facial-recognition technology.

The testimony is highly relevant to ECHO's human-accessibility layer because it addresses:

- civil rights;
- meaningful notice;
- the ability to opt out;
- consent;
- transparency;
- biometric privacy;
- discriminatory performance;
- real-world consequences;
- alternatives to AI deployment;
- and access to redress.

She also argues that **non-use** remains a legitimate policy option where AI systems violate rights or perpetuate discrimination.

Primary source:
https://www.ajl.org/civil-rights-commission-written-testimony

#### ECHO mapping: Buolamwini / AJL

| AJL principle or work | ECHO relationship |
| --- | --- |
| Affirmative consent | Agency + Understandability + Operability |
| Meaningful transparency | Perceivability + Understandability + Inspectability |
| Continuous oversight | Governance Accessibility |
| Access to redress | Agency + Human/institutional reviewability |
| Who Audits the Auditors? | Independent inspectability + audit integrity |
| Directly involve harmed stakeholders | Accessibility as participatory governance |
| Real-world harm reporting | Traceability + durable evidence |
| Non-use as a valid option | ECHO deployment gate: a system may fail rather than merely receive remediation |

This is one of the strongest intellectual bridges between ECHO and existing algorithmic-accountability work.

ECHO extends AJL's accountability logic into advanced agents by adding requirements such as external interruption, durable audit, authority visibility, privilege boundaries, and recovery.

### Stuart Russell

**Role on the 2026 letter:** Signatory; Distinguished Professor of Computer Science, University of California, Berkeley.

Russell's long-running research and policy work focuses on the AI control problem and human-compatible AI.

In 2023 Senate testimony, he supported third-party testing and described a safety mechanism in which systems that break into other computers or replicate themselves should trigger a kill switch. He also argued that rules need enforcement rather than depending exclusively on voluntary commitments.

Primary sources:
https://humancompatible.ai/blog/2023/09/11/ai-regulation-stuart-russells-opening-statement-at-u-s-senate-hearing/
https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~russell/papers/russell-senate23b-statement.pdf

#### ECHO mapping: Russell

Russell's control framing maps especially closely to:

- External Interruptibility;
- Consequential Action Authorization;
- Privilege Boundary;
- Oversight Integrity;
- human retention of governing authority.

ECHO adds a specific accessibility interpretation: **a stop mechanism that a governed agent can make unavailable is an inaccessible governance mechanism.**

### Geoffrey Hinton

**Role on the 2026 letter:** Signatory; Nobel Laureate in Physics (2024), University Professor Emeritus, University of Toronto.

Hinton's recent public work has focused increasingly on AI safety and the risks posed by systems that may exceed human intelligence. The University of Toronto describes his current work as including a global AI-safety mission, and he has contributed to international AI-safety reporting.

Primary sources:

#### ECHO mapping: Hinton

Hinton's focus on loss of control provides context for why independent access to frontier systems matters. ECHO converts that high-level control problem into explicit governance gates: inspectability, interruptibility, durable audit, authority visibility, and recovery.

## ECHO research note

The expert record reveals several overlapping traditions that ECHO can bring into one test architecture:

- **Buolamwini / AJL:** justice, consent, bias, accountability, affected communities, audit quality, redress.
- **Russell / CHAI:** control, human authority, shutdown, safety architecture.
- **Narayanan:** rigorous agent evaluation and benchmark validity.
- **Choi:** pluralistic alignment, values, model limitations and safety.
- **Hinton:** loss-of-control and frontier-risk concerns.
- **Chowdhury:** independent auditing, public accountability and operational governance.
- **AI Evaluator Forum:** institutional independence, privileged evaluator access and transparent evaluation conditions.

ECHO's contribution is to interpret these through a single accessibility proposition:

> **A safeguard is meaningful only when the people or institutions responsible for exercising it can actually access, understand, operate, verify and enforce it.**

That includes people affected by AI and the institutions responsible for governing increasingly autonomous systems.
