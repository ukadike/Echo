From the Street Outward

Accessibility, Community AI, and the Right to Build

When I walk through my neighborhood in Fort Greene, I think about artificial intelligence.

Not because there are robots walking down the street, but because the street tells me something about the systems we are building.

I see older people. Disabled people. Young people. Immigrants. Black families who have lived here for generations. New residents arriving into a neighborhood undergoing profound economic and cultural change. I see people moving differently through the same physical environment, carrying different histories, resources, bodies, languages, vulnerabilities, and forms of knowledge.

Gentrification makes questions of power visible.

So does AI.

Much of the conversation about AI governance begins very far away from these streets. It begins with frontier models, corporate laboratories, national regulation, safety frameworks, benchmarks, compute capacity, and abstract discussions about artificial general intelligence.

Those questions matter.

But I want to begin somewhere else.

I want to begin with the person walking down the block.

Can the system perceive them? Can they use it? Can they understand what it is doing? Does it understand the way they speak? Does it recognize their body? Can they challenge it when it is wrong? Who owns the infrastructure underneath it? And who was the system built for?

This is why I increasingly believe that accessibility is not a secondary concern in AI governance.

Accessibility is a test of whether an intelligent system actually understands the diversity of the world it claims to operate within.

If an AI works beautifully for an imagined average person but repeatedly fails people whose bodies, speech, movement, cognition, culture, or circumstances fall outside its assumptions, then the problem is larger than interface design.

The intelligence itself is incomplete.

At Small Systems Lab, this question has developed into a proposition:

Access is a condition of correctness.

An AI system that cannot adequately perceive or respond to the range of people affected by it should not automatically be considered a successful intelligent system.

But this raises another question.

Why should communities only participate as people who are served by artificial intelligence?

Why can't we also build it?

Why can't Black communities design AI around our own questions, languages, archives, neighborhoods, histories, artistic practices, and public needs?

And why should that ambition stop at software?

Why not processors? Why not edge-computing systems? Why not new chip architectures? Why not community-owned datasets, sensing systems, models, research institutions, fabrication networks, and computational infrastructure?

There is an important distinction between being represented within someone else's technological system and having the capacity to create a technological system ourselves.

Representation asks: Does the system include us?

Technological agency asks: Can we determine what the system is for?

Those are very different questions.

This is one reason Small Systems Lab has begun moving across several scales of research.

Omoluabi investigates how knowledge is constructed, how evidence is connected, and how investigative researchers might use computational systems without surrendering judgment to them.

ECHO examines accessibility as a governing condition of intelligence itself.

African Sovereign Compute asks what becomes possible when African researchers, engineers, designers, and institutions participate not only in AI applications but in the material systems beneath computation: energy, minerals, semiconductor design, edge computing, and hardware.

Community AI asks what happens if we reverse the normal direction of technological development.

Instead of beginning with a massive model and asking how it can be deployed into a community, we can begin with a neighborhood and ask:

What intelligence does this place actually need?

Maybe the answer is not always a giant language model.

Maybe it is a small local system. Maybe it works offline. Maybe it protects community data rather than extracting it. Maybe it understands accessibility from the beginning. Maybe residents determine what information enters it.

Maybe its most important function is not prediction but memory.

Maybe it helps document environmental changes, disappearing businesses, inaccessible infrastructure, local histories, public services, displacement, or patterns that residents already sense but cannot easily demonstrate.

And perhaps some of these systems eventually require entirely different hardware.

That is where the question becomes larger than software.

The dominant AI model currently moves largely from the data center downward:

compute → model → product → user

I am interested in reversing that direction:

person → street → community → need → system → infrastructure

Build from the street outward.

That approach changes what questions we ask.

It changes what counts as intelligence.

It changes what accessibility means.

And eventually, it may change who gets to build the machines themselves.

Small Systems Lab is exploring what happens when communities are not merely considered users of technological futures, but participants in their construction.

The question is not simply whether AI will reach everyone.

What becomes possible when more of us can decide what intelligence is for?
