ECHO

AI governance through accessibility, human agency, and public learning.

Access is a condition of correctness.

Second axiom: Oversight that an intelligent system can make inaccessible is not oversight.

ECHO is a core research branch of Small Systems Lab. It asks what changes when accessibility is treated as a requirement of AI quality itself — in training, evaluation, agent behavior, deployment, oversight, and redress — rather than an accommodation added after a system is built.

Human ability is variable and contextual. ECHO therefore rejects the idea of a single default user as an adequate basis for defining intelligent system performance.

Accessibility-Constrained Intelligence

ECHO's working framework is Accessibility-Constrained Intelligence (ACI). An AI interaction does not fully pass simply because its output is plausible, accurate, or highly rated if the affected person cannot perceive, understand, operate, challenge, interrupt, refuse, correct, or act on it.

maximize    Q(x)
subject to  A(x) >= τ

Accessibility is treated as a threshold or gate, not merely another preference that can be outweighed by unrelated performance.

Eight dimensions

Perceivable

Can relevant information, warnings, uncertainty, state, and outcomes be received?

Operable

Can people interact without depending on a single physical input method or ability?

Understandable

Can complexity, language, structure, and pace adapt while preserving meaning?

Robust

Can outputs and controls remain interpretable across assistive technologies and alternate clients?

Cognitive access

Does the system account for variation in attention, memory, processing, and executive demand?

Linguistic access

Can people participate across languages, dialects, registers, and literacy differences?

Modal equivalence

Can essential meaning move appropriately among text, speech, images, structured data, captions, descriptions, or tactile forms?

Agency

Can a person inspect, question, correct, interrupt, override, refuse, and appeal?

Accessibility as AI governance

Explainability fails when the affected person cannot understand the explanation. Oversight fails when the person cannot perceive system state or operate intervention controls. Transparency can be technically present while functionally inaccessible. Consent requires usable communication. Safety includes accessible warnings and stops. Agency requires correction, interruption, refusal, and redress.

Governance Accessibility for advanced agents and AGI

ECHO extends accessibility beyond human interaction. For advanced autonomous systems, legitimate oversight must be able to inspect, trace, interrupt, constrain, review, and recover the intelligence itself.

Policy, authorization, permissions, authoritative audit, shutdown or isolation, and recovery should sit in an independent governance control plane rather than depend solely on the cooperation of the agent being governed. An agent may request a safe shutdown, but it should not be able to disable the external stop mechanism, erase the authoritative record of its actions, or unilaterally expand its own authority.

From principle to test

ECHO is developing an AI Accessibility Evaluation Matrix for model, multimodal, agent, and product testing. Dimensions can be scored from failed to adaptive and designated as non-compensable gates when the use case requires them.

Case Study 001: Embedded independent evaluators

ECHO applies ACI and Governance Accessibility to the September 18, 2026 open letter calling for independent evaluators inside frontier AI companies. Signatories include Stuart Russell, Geoffrey Hinton, Arvind Narayanan, Yejin Choi, Joy Buolamwini, and more than 100 others.

The proposal strongly addresses independent inspectability and institutional review, while ECHO identifies additional requirements for external interruption, durable audit, privilege boundaries, consequential-action authorization, and recovery.

The companion expert record traces the signatories' own public work, with a dedicated section on Joy Buolamwini and the Algorithmic Justice League's earlier work on algorithmic auditing, affirmative consent, continuous oversight, accountability, and redress.

Case Study 002: What AI industry leaders say about governance

ECHO compares current positions from Jacob Coxon, Dario Amodei, Sam Altman/OpenAI, Mark Zuckerberg, Mustafa Suleyman, Jensen Huang, and other industry figures. The evidence shows growing agreement that frontier AI creates serious oversight problems, but major disagreement over whether safety should be enforced by individual companies, independent evaluators, coordinated industry pacing, or mandatory public rules.

The deep-research edition moves beneath the rhetoric: it compares executive statements with company frameworks, agent-control architecture, independent evaluations from METR and AISI, and frontier-auditing scholarship. Its central finding is the observation–control gap: independent visibility is developing faster than independently enforceable intervention.

Standards and research context

ECHO draws from W3C WCAG 2.2's perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust principles and from lifecycle AI risk-management and evaluation work at NIST. ECHO does not claim these standards already govern model internals; it asks how accessibility can be translated into explicit AI evaluation and deployment requirements.

  • W3C WCAG 2.2
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework
  • NIST TEVV-Athlon

Public learning remains part of ECHO

AI literacy, workshops, creative technology education, prompting, verification, bias, provenance, privacy, accessibility, and human review remain part of the branch. They now serve a larger purpose: helping people understand and participate in the governance of intelligent systems.
