Don't Recognize Disability. Recognize Access.

Community AI and the Intelligence of Human Variation

When I walk beside Fort Greene Park, I see a street full of different ways of moving through the world.

A person using a wheelchair. Someone in a motorized chair. Me with my walker. A mother pushing a stroller. An older person walking slowly. A runner moving quickly. Someone carrying bags. Someone limping. Someone stopping to rest.

We are not separate populations. We are sharing the same environment.

That observation changes the way I think about AI governance.

Computer vision already knows how to estimate movement, pose, gesture, direction, speed, and relationships between bodies and objects. Years ago, I used facial recognition as one multimodal input for a thumb piano. That was basic machine learning: the body became part of the interface.

What if the environment could perceive changing access conditions without trying to identify or classify the people experiencing them?

The system should not ask: What kind of person is this?

It should ask: What access conditions are present here right now?

A wheelchair is not an identity. A walker is not an identity. A stroller is not an identity. Moving slowly, carrying groceries, recovering from surgery, navigating poor lighting, processing unfamiliar language, needing more time, or needing more space are all access conditions.

Human access is variable, contextual, and dynamic.

An access-state model

Let the local access state at time t be:

A(t) = [m, p, r, v, c, l, e, g]
  • m — mobility and path support
  • p — pace and timing support
  • r — reach and physical interaction support
  • v — perceptual and sensory support
  • c — cognitive-load support
  • l — language and communication support
  • e — environmental support
  • g — agency, consent, and control

Each value represents a probability or degree of need, not a diagnosis or permanent label.

A simplified inference model is:

P(A(t) | S(t), E(t))

S(t) represents minimal, local, non-identifying signals. E(t) represents the environment: curb height, slope, crowding, light, noise, traffic timing, weather, available seating, route conditions, and similar factors.

Community AI then chooses an action that improves participation:

x*(t) = arg maxx E[ Participation(x | A(t), E(t)) ]

Subject to:

Access(x) ≥ τA
Privacy(x) ≥ τP
Agency(x) ≥ τG
Identity inference = 0, by default

The optimization target is participation, not classification. The system does not need to decide who is disabled. It needs to determine whether the shared environment is currently usable.

Why the average is not enough

A community system should not average everybody into one “typical user.” If nine people can use a curb and one person cannot, the curb does not become accessible because the average person can cross it.

Community AI therefore needs a way to preserve less-common access requirements instead of averaging them away.

Cj(t) = Qq{A1j(t), A2j(t), …, Anj(t)}

Here, Qq represents a high quantile rather than a mean.

Design for the access conditions that matter, even when they are not the majority condition.

What this could mean on a street

A crossing system could notice that people currently using the intersection require more crossing time and extend it.

A navigation system could understand that the shortest route is not necessarily the most usable route.

A community robot could understand when its own position blocks a wheelchair, walker, stroller, or delivery cart.

A neighborhood system could notice repeated difficulty at the same curb, entrance, slope, or transit stop and convert recurring access failures into evidence for infrastructure repair.

The intelligence does not need to conclude: “This person is disabled.” It can conclude: “This path is currently failing some of the people trying to use it.”

The governance principle

The system must not turn accessibility into surveillance.

Recognition does not equal permission.

Community AI should therefore prefer local inference over centralized surveillance; anonymous or ephemeral signals over persistent identity; minimum necessary sensing; visible community rules; opt-in personalization where possible; uncertainty rather than false certainty; human challenge and override; and community control over what is sensed, retained, and acted upon.

The community should govern the sensing.

The AI should adapt downward toward the people using the environment rather than extracting information upward into systems they do not control.

From accessibility feature to intelligence model

ECHO asks: Did the AI learn that variation in human access is normal?

That question moves accessibility upstream. It changes training data, computer vision, robotics, navigation, public infrastructure, and what an AI system is optimized to notice.

At Small Systems Lab, the proposition is:

Access is a condition of correctness.

Community AI extends that proposition into place:

A community system is not intelligent if the people sharing that environment cannot meaningfully participate in it.

Don't recognize disability. Recognize access.

The community becomes an interface.

Build from the street outward.
